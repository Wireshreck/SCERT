# Chapter 6 — Classification

## Chapter overview

Classification organizes organisms by shared characteristics and is revised as scientific knowledge changes. Carl Woese’s work is used to explain the three-domain system: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya, and the separation of Archaea from bacteria. The six kingdoms shown are Bacteria, Archaea, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. The diagram contrasts bacterial cell walls containing peptidoglycan with archaeal walls without it and gives example organisms.

Animal classification is introduced through structural features such as body symmetry, germ layers and body cavities, with phyla examples in the textbook tables. Plant groups are considered through their distinguishing traits and relationships. The chapter notes that viruses do not fit the six-kingdom scheme: they lack cellular structures and cannot multiply without a host cell, though virus taxonomy has its own families, genera and species.

An evolutionary tree displays branching relationships and shared ancestral features. The book constructs an example using skull, forelimbs, hair/fur and lactation across lung fish, wall lizard, dog and human. DNA barcoding identifies species by comparing selected DNA sequences and supports modern biodiversity research. The chapter closes by linking classification to ecosystem relationships, conservation and recognizing species before they disappear.

## Chapter topics

Three-domain/six-kingdom classification; animal phyla and vertebrate classes; plant divisions; naming and describing newly discovered organisms; virus structure and classification; evolutionary trees; DNA barcoding; biodiversity and conservation.
