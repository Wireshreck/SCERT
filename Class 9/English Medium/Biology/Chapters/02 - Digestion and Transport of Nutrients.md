# Chapter 2 — Digestion and Transport of Nutrients

## Chapter focus

This chapter follows food from breakdown into absorbable molecules to its movement through the body. It treats digestion and absorption as linked processes, explains how blood and lymph distribute nutrients, and compares animal circulation with transport through plant vascular tissues.

## Main ideas

### Digestion and absorption

The chapter revisits nutrient classes and the enzymes that act on them. Its diagrams and assessment tasks distinguish digestion of carbohydrates, proteins and fats; lipase is identified in the assessment as the enzyme associated with fat digestion. The small intestine is presented as the key absorption site. Its villi increase absorptive surface and contain blood capillaries and a lacteal, so absorbed nutrients do not all follow the same route.

Water-soluble nutrients such as glucose and amino acids enter blood capillaries, reach the liver through the hepatic portal vein, leave through the hepatic vein and return toward the heart. Fatty-acid and glycerol products enter lacteals and lymph vessels before joining circulation. The chapter’s fill-in flowchart and labelled transport illustration ask learners to reconstruct those routes.

### Human circulation

The heart is a muscular pump in the thoracic cavity, protected by the rib cage and pericardium; pericardial fluid reduces friction and cushions it. The heart diagram labels four chambers and the venae cavae, pulmonary vessels, aorta and valves. Valves direct flow through the chambers. The cardiac cycle is shown as atrial systole, ventricular systole and joint diastole; the book states a cycle takes 0.8 seconds. The SA node in the right atrium produces impulses that regulate rhythmic contraction and is called the pacemaker.

The text defines pulse through expansion and recoil of arterial walls associated with ventricular contraction. A paired measurement activity compares pulse and heartbeat at rest and after exercise. Blood pressure is introduced through systolic and diastolic phases; the table gives 120 mmHg and 80 mmHg respectively as the illustrated values. These values are textbook examples, not individual diagnostic guidance.

Blood transports oxygen, nutrients and other materials. The double-circulation diagram links the heart, lungs and body organs, and end questions ask why blood does not flow back from ventricles to atria and why double circulation is useful.

### Transport in plants

Xylem and phloem are compared visually. The book describes xylem tracheids and vessels as water-conducting tissues and phloem sieve tubes with companion cells as food-conducting tissues. Water enters roots by osmosis; root pressure contributes, while transpiration from leaves and cohesion/adhesion help maintain upward water movement. Photosynthetic glucose is transferred to sieve tubes as sucrose using energy; water entry creates turgor pressure that supports food conduction.

- Villi and nutrient-route illustrations distinguish blood-capillary and lacteal transport (printed pp. 35–37).
- Heart-protection and chamber diagrams identify the pericardium, chambers, major vessels and valves (pp. 37–40).
- Cardiac-cycle and ECG figures accompany questions about heartbeat regulation (pp. 40–41).
- Table 2.3 asks students to compare heartbeat and pulse at rest and after exercise; Table 2.4 pairs systole/diastole with textbook blood-pressure examples (pp. 42–43).
- Vascular-tissue and plant-water-conduction diagrams compare xylem and phloem and trace water upward (pp. 47–49).
- End-of-chapter questions use circulation diagrams, nutrient flowcharts and a tooth section; extended activities ask for heart-health awareness and a local nutritious-food event (pp. 50–52).
