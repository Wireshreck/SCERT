# Chapter 3 — Respiration and Excretion

## Chapter focus

The final chapter links gas exchange and cellular energy release with removal of metabolic wastes. It follows gases from respiratory surfaces into blood and tissues, contrasts respiration with breathing, and explains how kidneys form urine while contributing to homeostasis. It also considers plant excretion, haemodialysis and transplantation.

## Main ideas

### Respiration and gas exchange

The chapter opens with Lavoisier’s historical comparison between combustion and organismal respiration, then examines the human respiratory system. The respiratory surface must support effective diffusion: the assessment highlights a thin, moist surface, close blood-capillary supply and large surface area. The alveolus/capillary illustration and questions about oxygen and carbon-dioxide concentration guide learners to explain gas exchange. Plasma and red blood cells transport gases; haemoglobin is specifically queried as part of oxygen transport.

Respiration is considered at cellular level as well as at the organ level. The topic list includes cellular respiration and respiration in other organisms, encouraging comparison of how organisms obtain energy and exchange gases. The printed diagrams and exercises ask students to label the trachea, lungs and diaphragm and distinguish pulmonary and systemic circulation.

### Excretion and kidneys

Excretion removes metabolic wastes and supports homeostasis. The human urinary diagram identifies kidney, ureter, urinary bladder and urethra. The nephron illustration presents urine formation in three stages:

1. **Ultrafiltration:** blood pressure in the glomerulus produces glomerular filtrate; the differing diameters of afferent and efferent vessels contribute to the pressure.
2. **Reabsorption:** essential components are returned from the renal tubule to blood.
3. **Secretion:** excess components are moved from blood into the tubule.

The text lists water, glucose, amino acids, ions, vitamins and wastes among the filtrate components, then explains that urine contains water and wastes such as urea, salts, uric acid and creatinine after selective reabsorption and secretion. Urine passes through collecting ducts to the pelvis, then through ureter to bladder and leaves by urethra. The chapter’s urine-analysis table connects abnormal findings such as glucose, albumin, blood, bilirubin, calcium oxalate crystals and pus cells with possible conditions; it is an educational table, not a standalone diagnostic guide.

### Kidney support and plant excretion

A haemodialysis diagram shows blood entering a dialysis unit with anticoagulant, wastes diffusing across selectively permeable tubing into dialysis fluid, and cleaned blood returning after anticoagulant reversal. Kidney transplantation is presented as another treatment context. The chapter also includes excretion in plants and returns to homeostasis as a unifying idea: exchange, transport, energy-releasing reactions and timely waste removal jointly keep internal conditions suitable for life.

## Figures, activities and assessment

- Respiratory-system diagram labels the trachea, lungs and diaphragm (printed p. 77).
- Gas-exchange prompts ask learners to compare alveolar and capillary conditions and explain roles of plasma, RBCs, haemoglobin and tissue fluid.
- Urinary-system diagram labels the kidney, ureter, bladder and urethra (p. 77).
- Nephron diagram and worksheet show ultrafiltration, reabsorption, secretion and urine pathway (pp. 68–69).
- A Benedict-reagent activity uses colour change to investigate glucose in a urine sample; the text asks students to record observations and inferences (p. 70).
- The haemodialysis illustration traces the blood and dialysis-fluid pathways (p. 71).
- End-of-chapter assessments include identifying an inefficient gas-exchange feature, labelling organs and analysing pulmonary/systemic circulation (pp. 77–80).
