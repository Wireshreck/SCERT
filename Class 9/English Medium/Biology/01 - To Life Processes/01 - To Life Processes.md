# Chapter 1 — To Life Processes

## Chapter focus

The chapter connects cellular chemistry to the needs of organisms. It introduces the molecules cells use, the reactions that sustain life, the exchange of materials across membranes, and photosynthesis as a source of food and oxygen. It also emphasizes the ecological and economic services plants provide.

## Topics in the textbook

- Biomolecules and metabolism
- Internal environment and homeostasis
- Plasma membrane and exchange of substances
- Photosynthesis and nutrients
- Plant services and plant protection

## Main ideas

### Biomolecules and metabolism

Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids form the basic molecular groups discussed. Their examples include glucose and starch; fats and oils; enzymes and antibodies; and DNA and RNA. Metabolism is the sum of chemical reactions in an organism. Anabolism combines smaller molecules into larger ones; catabolism breaks molecules down. Enzymes speed reactions, while hormones coordinate biological processes.

### Internal environment and exchange

Cells receive substances from their surroundings through the plasma membrane. In animals, extracellular fluid between cells is the internal environment; the chapter describes plant internal conditions in terms of cell walls and their components, extracellular fluid, and air spaces. Homeostasis is maintaining a relatively constant internal composition so metabolism can proceed.

The plasma membrane is selectively permeable. The salt-solution and fresh-water leaf investigation and the beaker diagram introduce osmosis: water moves through a semipermeable membrane from a region of higher water concentration to lower water concentration. The paired cell drawings show water gain and loss in plant cells. Oxygen, carbon dioxide and water can cross the membrane; some substances and ions use specific channels.

### Photosynthesis and nutrients

The chloroplast discussion and photosynthesis diagrams connect light energy and chlorophyll to the conversion of carbon dioxide and water into glucose, with oxygen released. Glucose may be stored as starch and can supply material for other nutrients. The assessment and activity prompts ask learners to identify reactants and products and investigate the role of light; they are inquiry tasks, not claims that every suggested experiment has a fixed outcome.

Plants are autotrophs and provide nutrients to heterotrophs through food relationships. The illustrated examples show carbohydrate sources (including rice, wheat, tubers, sugarcane, citrus and jackfruit), fat sources, pulses and vegetables. The ocean-zone illustration distinguishes euphotic, dysphotic and aphotic zones by available light and connects photosynthesis to aquatic food webs. It notes chemosynthetic bacteria in deep zones where photosynthesis does not occur.

### Plant services and conservation

Figures connect plants with latex, medicines, biopesticides, spices, tea and coffee. A mangrove panel emphasizes coastal soil protection, fish resources and biodiversity, and the book gives a historical estimate of mangrove loss in Kerala. Learners are asked to organize a seminar on ecological and economic importance and local conservation. The central conclusion is that plant depletion threatens the biosphere and that sustainability requires environmental awareness.

## Figures, activities and assessment

- Biomolecule classification diagram (printed p. 9) groups examples into four molecular classes.
- Cell-exchange diagrams compare unicellular organisms, animal extracellular fluid and plant cell pathways (pp. 10–11).
- Plasma-membrane drawing labels the membrane between internal environment and cytoplasm; a leaf-in-water/salt investigation and water-flow diagram support osmosis (pp. 12–13).
- Photosynthesis phase table and reactant/product flow diagram invite learners to connect conditions and products (pp. 17–18).
- Nutrient-source and ocean-zone illustrations connect nutrition with ecosystems (pp. 19–21).
- Learner assessment asks about protecting plants; extended prompts include local biodiversity and plant conservation (end of chapter, p. 24).
